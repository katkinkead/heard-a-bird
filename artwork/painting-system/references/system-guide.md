# Birdsong-to-Art: the reusable painting system

**Approved foundation: the 75-second Robin, 20 October 2026.**

The aim is a coherent collection of 40–100 birds, each recognisable and individual, with the same material richness and considered arrival. The common system is **visual language + material behavior + a painting score**. The bird supplies the anatomy, palette, personality and movement.

The shared foundation is:

> Recognisable species + considered individual expression + layered pigment and precise drawn detail + an open, simple abstract composition + a 75-second, anatomically choreographed arrival.

The Robin is the approved motion standard. It is not a silhouette to recolour for other species. The current art anchors establish the family; they do not settle every species' final artwork.

For finished portraits, use the dedicated [final-art system](final-art-system.md), recorded after inspecting all five original designs on 21 October 2026. It develops the visual rules below independently of animation timing, including the later user-endorsed Woodpigeon v2 refinement: clear pigment masses, rhythmic feather groups and selective precision. Read its comparison before new concept work; the approved motion baseline remains separate.

## The visual language

Use these descriptors together:

> Expressive ornithological illustration; restrained caricature; cold-pressed ivory paper; translucent watercolour underpainting; rich gouache-like pigment; clear pigment masses; rhythmic feather groups; selective structural contrast; dry-brush print grain; fine navy ink contours; sharp coloured-pencil feather work; subtle colour misregistration; selective intricacy, with engraved feather or leaf-vein marks as optional examples; generous exposed paper; understated handwritten naming.

“Whimsical”, “magical”, “quirky” and “cute” alone are too vague: they led toward the generic, decorative character art the user rejected. The eccentricity should come from a specific visual decision.

| Keep consistent | Adapt for each bird |
|---|---|
| Paper, pigment, line and detail language | Species anatomy and diagnostic plumage |
| Bird-dominant composition and exposed paper | Facing direction, pose and diagonal rhythm |
| Considered, recognisable character | Coordinated posture, gaze, proportion and gesture |
| A carefully drawn finish with quiet passages | Detail location, density and technique; decorative motif optional |
| Simple abstract support for the bird | Shape vocabulary, number, overlap, placement and colour; no geometry also valid |
| Understated name and tiny corner annotation | Accurate species label and truthful activity/history |
| Sketch → water → pigment → structure → detail | The route through the bird and the late finishing feature |

Caricature should be evident in silhouette or posture before accessories. A clear emphasis can help, but it is not a quota; coordinated expressive choices are welcome. Preserve recognisability as proportions and posture vary. Keep the Robin's breast close to natural proportions. Preserve bill shape, tail structure, wing pattern and other identifying markings. Expressive posture is an artistic choice, not evidence of a species' psychological personality.

The palette family includes navy/petrol, turquoise, cobalt, vermilion/orange, ochre/saffron, coral and warm ivory. These are shared pigment options, not a requirement to put every colour on every bird. Real identifying colours determine the dominant palette.

Retain both softness and precision: blooms and broken pigment underneath, crisp feather shafts, eye/beak definition and delicate engraved lines in the finish. A uniformly soft watercolour loses the richness the user approved; a uniformly sharp engraving loses the painting.

## Background grammar

The established portraits use **one flat shape** with broken print or dry-brush edges. It sits behind the bird and borrows from, or gently counterbalances, the palette. It is not a landscape, halo system or scene.

| Existing bird | Shape | Palette and placement |
|---|---|---|
| Robin | Large circle | Turquoise, behind the body |
| Blackbird | Broad rectangle | Warm ochre, behind the body |
| Blue tit | Semicircle with a flat lower edge | Coral, offset left/up |
| Wren | Horizontal rectangle | Pale teal, behind the body |
| Goldfinch | Narrow vertical rectangle | Pale ochre, offset left behind the head/shoulder |

The Goldfinch's earlier turquoise circle was rejected for resembling the Robin too closely. These are examples of successful compositions, not a closed vocabulary. New work may use no geometry, one form or several simple forms: arcs, bands, loose polygons, overlapping washes and other abstract inventions are welcome. Judge simplicity and visual hierarchy rather than shape count.

For a larger collection, reuse or extend the background vocabulary as each composition needs; review for both coherence and individuality. Keep a simple visual contact sheet or species catalogue to check repetition. A minimal sweeping perch is enough when a perch is needed. No scenic garden, castle, elaborate foliage or architectural background.

## The arrival is a painting performance

The drawing should feel like a short melodic performance: a long searching gesture, a fuller press of the brush, a tapering lift, pigment continuing to travel, then a return with a sharper instrument. The intermediate drawing should look intentional.

| Approximate screen time in the approved Robin | What the viewer sees |
|---|---|
| 0–10 seconds | Light gesture and structure; the bird becomes legible |
| 10–20 | Local water followed by the first pigment contact |
| 20–30 | Pigment travels, overlaps and begins to establish colour |
| 30–40 | More loaded, directional strokes strengthen the colour masses |
| 40–50 | The head, contour and breast acquire form |
| 50–60 | Belly, back, tail and wing structure develop |
| 60–70 | Wing layers and pattern progress; feet and perch begin to resolve |
| 70–75 | Substantial remaining wing/foot/perch detail, final accents, name and small activity mark |

These are overlapping phrases, not hard global stage boundaries. The precise Robin timing is frozen in the implementation reference. A Wren should retain the same sense of progress without being forced into the Robin's anatomy or exact routes.

Material rules:

1. **Sketch with intention.** Use a few meaningful contours and construction gestures. Avoid a technical wireframe or an instant complete outline.
2. **Place water locally.** Suggest a temporary, restrained sheen. Keep most of the paper dry.
3. **Let pigment move after contact.** Colour enters the wet region, spreads, slows and deposits. The effect stays inside the intended anatomy.
4. **Show pressure.** A brush's belly broadens at a press; movement eases; paint load changes the deposit; lifting tapers the mark. Pressure, water and pigment concentration are distinct controls.
5. **Build overlapping passages.** Visit anatomical regions in phrases. Within each region, preserve broad → medium → fine so a later coarse stroke does not cover finished detail.
6. **Finish sharply.** Settled washes receive fine coloured-pencil and ink-like work. Preserve the final image's richness.

These are art-directed material approximations with compressed drying time, not a claim that a real wash dries in seconds or a calibrated fluid simulation.

## Progress is perceptual

The approved improvement was not simply “90 seconds played faster”. Large visible paint changes were given more time; low-impact finishing was compressed. At 65 seconds the Robin's patterned wing still reads as unfinished and the feet/perch remain unresolved.

Judge every ten-second comparison at the size the art will actually be viewed. Look for a new colour passage, visible form, feather group or meaningful edge. Then watch uninterrupted playback to check that those changes arrive fluidly.

Do not spend the ending moving tiny pixels while the bird already looks finished. Do not make an eye glint, a signature or background completion carry the entire last ten seconds. Do not count marks as a proxy for progress: the approved Robin places many tiny marks very quickly at the end.

## Motion variation on accepted artwork

This section concerns motion variations on an already accepted illustration; it does not limit creative choices for new finished art. Use a few authored motion alternatives, selected with a recorded seed, rather than uncontrolled random changes. A variation can alter the initial sweep, the order of two independent anatomical passages, a pigment entry point, or the location of one small engraved feather accent.

A useful starting convention is **one major motion variation and one small flourish** per rendition. This is a proposed production convention, not a separately approved quota. Keep the silhouette, identifying markings, material sequence, detailed finish and perceptual pacing stable. Never create novelty by distorting anatomy, inserting costumes, adding text jokes or abandoning the shared palette logic.

## Naming and states

The user removed fun facts and humorous captions. Do not reintroduce them. Keep the species name below the bird, with restrained handwritten character.

The live concept uses an extremely small lower-right mark: a central dot inside full circular ripples seen in perspective, appearing as flattened ellipses, with “Singing now”. It is not a Wi-Fi fan. The accepted source contains this text as an illustration example; a production system must render state from actual evidence, independently of the painting animation.

Do not add “Listening available” to the portrait. Listening capability and bird activity use different symbols. A disconnected Pi uses the small unplugged pastry-pie symbol already requested. Idle birds can show monthly repertoire, last heard, or local-but-not-yet-heard status; never invent counts. Preparing and queued states stay off the art. Any progress indicator should be a subtle filling brushstroke representing the drawing, not fabricated generation progress.

## What happens next

The existing Goldfinch, Wren, Blackbird and Blue tit have now received 75-second motion adaptations; the original Robin remains the explicitly approved benchmark. The current task is to establish the final-art system before developing additional species concepts. Review and accept each new finished image before animating it. Documentation and successful adaptations of existing art do not themselves validate new artwork for 40–100 birds.
